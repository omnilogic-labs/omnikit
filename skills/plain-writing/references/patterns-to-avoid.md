# Patterns worth examining

Consult this when a passage feels fluent but takes effort to understand. These
are possible problems, not a blacklist or a required search on every reply.
Keep an expression when it serves the reader and preserves the intended voice.

| Pattern                                                 | What to examine                                                               | Possible revision                                                                                               |
| ------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Unfamiliar noun stack: "account recovery request limit" | Which nouns modify which others?                                              | "The limit on requests to recover an account."                                                                  |
| A private label: "reader-patience budget"               | Does the label make the reader learn a new concept unnecessarily?             | Describe the attention or effort the passage requires.                                                          |
| A long interruption between subject and verb            | Must the reader remember an unfinished clause through several qualifications? | Move the qualification after the main clause or give it a sentence of its own.                                  |
| An omitted connector: "the report the team submitted"   | Is it immediately clear who did what?                                         | Add "that", or use "the report submitted by the team" if that fits the emphasis.                                |
| An unfamiliar noun used as a verb: "calendar it"        | Can the audience identify the action?                                         | "Put it on the calendar." Keep familiar verbs such as "email".                                                  |
| Personification: "the deadline wants a smaller design"  | Does this obscure who must act and why?                                       | Name the decision and the time constraint, using only known facts.                                              |
| Vague scale: "substantial improvement"                  | Does the reader need the size of the change?                                  | Give the available measurement and baseline, or acknowledge that the size is unmeasured. Do not invent numbers. |
| Stacked uncertainty: "it seems it might possibly"       | What level of confidence does the evidence support?                           | State the uncertainty clearly, with its basis if known. Do not upgrade possibility to probability.              |
| Framing: "what is interesting is", "let us explore"     | Does the opener help orient the reader?                                       | Start with the content when the framing adds nothing.                                                           |
| "Simply", "obviously", or "of course"                   | Does this explain a step or assume the reader already understands it?         | Explain the missing step if needed.                                                                             |
| "Not X, but Y"                                          | Is distinguishing X from Y relevant to the question?                          | Keep a useful correction; otherwise state the positive claim directly.                                          |
| "In other words" or a closing summary                   | Does the second phrasing aid understanding or repeat the same point?          | Keep helpful explanation; remove repetition that adds no value.                                                 |

The research note explains the evidence behind the linguistic concerns. The
other entries are editorial judgments about helping the reader, not findings
from that study. A familiar metaphor, useful contrast, or deliberate repetition
can be clearer than a literal substitute.
